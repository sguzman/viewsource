# Viewsource Constitution

## Article I — Independent public scope

Viewsource studies websites as versioned research specimens. Its outputs are original technical explanations, a machine-readable ontology, a human-readable dossier, and curated research tasks. Its normal operation requires no unpublished repository, private service, private ontology, or other hidden dependency. The project maintains its own ontology and gallery.

## Article II — Public information boundary

Never put private chats, messages, prompts, transcripts, notes, venting, private tone, secrets, identifying details, unpublished research, confidential paths or metadata, or paraphrases revealing these sources into public documentation, source, comments, tests, fixtures, issue bodies, branches, commits, workflows, logs, releases, galleries, or Git history. All public work uses neutral professional technical language. Exclude anything whose provenance, release rights, or public suitability is uncertain. Scanners supplement but do not substitute for source and rights review. A later deletion does not remove exposed content from history.

## Article III — Capture isolation and third-party rights

Saved pages, ZIPs, full markup, downloaded scripts, CSS bundles, media, fonts, network traffic exports, cookies, browser state, session data, and derived copies of third-party source files are **temporary local evidence only**. They must never be added to repository content or Git history, even when a site is public. Captures must be processed offline by default without executing untrusted scripts. Public output may include original abstractions, carefully minimized technical identifiers when cleared, evidence classification, and verified high-level findings. Do not automatically publish captured URLs or identifiers that might contain secrets or user data.

## Article IV — Evidentiary integrity

Separate directly observed content from static inference, external corroboration, and unavailable facts. A flat copy is valid evidence for structural and source-level inspection but cannot prove server implementations, live API responses, real user behavior, hidden pages, or security claims without further evidence. Never require live crawling to accept a specimen. Record uncertainty and limitations explicitly. No fabricated screenshots, findings, tests, or completed tasks.

## Article V — Ontology and gallery

Keep versioned, documented machine-readable types and human-readable definitions in \`ontology/\`. Define stable identities, attributes, relationships, sources, confidence, and evidence semantics. Store per-specimen immutable version dossiers under \`specimens/\`. Maintain a truthful human-readable gallery and machine-readable \`gallery/index.json\`, which may remain empty until independently publishable material exists.

## Article VI — Research lifecycle

The owner selects specimen identities. Intake of a new capture creates a new internal version; old results remain separately addressable. Task graph generation is deterministic, tracks dependencies and topical queues, and cannot assert completion. Findings are only promoted after analysis and source/rights review. Version comparisons distinguish observed changes from differences in capture scope.

## Article VII — Tarium governance

Taria refers to the private ecosystem; Tarium to the separate public registry and its explicitly enrolled independent projects. A public project, use of Tarium templates, or an intention to become a Tarium member does **not** authorize registry changes. Only the owner may give a direct and unambiguous instruction to enroll this named repository in the central registry. Do not edit the Tarium hub without that distinct authorization. Another Tarium member may be referenced as such only when it is publicly available and actually registered. Never reference or require private Taria resources.

## Article VIII — Publication, enforcement, amendment

Before each public change, inspect file paths, contents, dependencies, issue/commit messages, generated outputs, and provenance. Exclude unresolved third-party material and private evidence. Run local validation and publication guards. Stop further publication if a leak is suspected; assess the affected history and rotate credentials if necessary. The owner controls substantive constitutional changes. Downstream rules may strengthen, not weaken, these protections.
